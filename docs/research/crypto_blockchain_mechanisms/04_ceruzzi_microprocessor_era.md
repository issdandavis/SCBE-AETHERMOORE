# 04 — Ceruzzi, *Computing: A History of the Information Machine*: Four Threads and the Microprocessor Era

Source: Paul E. Ceruzzi, *Computing: A Concise History* (MIT Press Essential
Knowledge, 2012). The session called it "Computing: A History of the Information
Machine." **⚠ Verify the exact title and edition.** Ceruzzi also wrote *A History of
Modern Computing* (MIT Press, 2nd ed. 2003). All descriptions below are **paraphrase**.

## Four threads Ceruzzi uses to organize the history
1. **Digitization**: more and more of the world gets represented as discrete numbers.
2. **Convergence**: separate technologies (calculation, communication, control,
   media) merge into one digital platform.
3. **Moore's Law**: steady exponential growth in transistor density drives cost
   down and capability up.
4. **The human–machine interface**: from switches and cards, to terminals, to GUIs,
   to touch and voice. Who can use a computer keeps expanding.

## Chapter map (per session, paraphrase)
- **Ch. 2, the first computers, 1935–45**: Zuse, Atanasoff–Berry, Harvard Mark I,
  Colossus and ENIAC. These were electromechanical and electronic machines built
  mainly for war-related calculation.
- **Ch. 3, the stored-program principle**: EDVAC, EDSAC and the von Neumann
  architecture (see 02).
- **Microprocessor chapter**:
  - **Altair 8800**: MITS, introduced January 1975 (*Popular Electronics* cover),
    built on the Intel 8080.
  - **IMSAI 8080**: an Altair-compatible competitor.
  - **Processor Technology Sol-20**: an early all-in-one with keyboard and video.
  - **Apple**: Steve Wozniak designed the Apple I (1976) and Apple II (1977). Steve
    Jobs drove the business and the product polish.
  - **Microsoft**: Bill Gates and Paul Allen began with Altair BASIC (1975). In the
    1980s, MS-DOS licensing on the IBM PC made Microsoft the dominant software platform.

## Thread to this project

The **human–machine interface** thread is the aggregator thesis (06). Each era
widened who could use computing by hiding complexity behind a simpler surface. Crypto
exchanges today are like the Altair: powerful, fragmented and technical. The
opportunity is the "Apple II moment," a front end that non-technical users can
operate.
